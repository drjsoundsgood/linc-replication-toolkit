# 01 · Why LiNC

## The problem: a leaky pipeline

Students from traditionally excluded groups express interest in STEM at rates comparable to their peers, yet they leave STEM at disproportionately higher rates, especially across the transition from high school to college ([Riegle-Crumb *et al.*, 2019](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X19831006)). The causes are well documented: systemic gatekeeping, isolated training environments, and an unwritten curriculum that rewards students who already know how academia works ([Hinton *et al.*, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33125882/)).

This loss matters for science itself. Demographic diversity drives scientific innovation ([Hofstra *et al.*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1915378117)), so every talented student who leaves is a loss to the field, not only to that student.

Many high school research programs exist, but most are short and isolated. A student spends a few weeks shadowing, receives a certificate, and has no ongoing connection to a lab, a mentor or a peer network. That model can spark interest, but it rarely builds the **research identity, skills and relationships** that carry a student through college.

## The LiNC response

LiNC treats a high school student as a **junior member of the lab** for several years, not as a summer visitor. The program is built on five principles.

### 1. Start early, stay long
Recruit in 9th or 10th grade, before students have decided STEM "isn't for them," and bring them back every summer until they start college. A student who joins after 9th grade can complete three or four summers. Each return builds on the last.

### 2. Real autonomy
Students are trained for about two weeks, then run their own experiments. They manage a real research budget, decide what to buy, make mistakes, troubleshoot, and propose next steps. Ownership, not observation, is what builds confidence.

### 3. Authentic research
Projects are open questions whose answers the lab genuinely needs. Results go into manuscripts, and students who contribute are co-authors. Students know their work matters because it does.

### 4. Mentors come in all shapes and sizes
Every member of the lab mentors, from the PI to undergraduates. Returning LiNC students train incoming ones. Students see people at every career stage, including people only a year or two ahead of them.

### 5. Support doesn't end in August
The program helps students choose colleges, write essays, and find a research lab at the university they attend. Alumni stay connected to the lab and to each other.

## Why it's good for your lab, too

LiNC is not only service. Programs built this way:

- **Generate preliminary data** for lab projects, including tests of computational predictions that might otherwise sit untested.
- **Give graduate students and postdocs real mentoring experience**, which strengthens fellowship applications and job packets.
- **Build a lab culture** of teaching, explaining and inclusion that benefits every member.
- **Strengthen broader impacts** sections of grant proposals with concrete, measurable outcomes.
- **Create a recruitment pipeline** for future undergraduate researchers.

## What success looks like

Define success before you start, so you can measure it (see [Assessment](13-assessment.md)). Reasonable targets for a program at steady state:

| Outcome | Target |
|---|---|
| Returning students (among those still in high school) | Near 100% year over year |
| Students who produce their own experimental data | 100% |
| Gains in experimental confidence and lab belonging (pre vs. post) | Positive and statistically significant across cohorts |
| Alumni entering college in a STEM or life sciences major | High; track every alumnus |
| Alumni doing undergraduate research | Track by end of first college year |
| Publications with student co-authors | At least one per project cycle |

## Is this right for your lab?

LiNC works best when:

- Your lab has **projects with modular, teachable experiments** that a trained high school student can run safely.
- You have **at least two or three people** (postdocs, graduate students, undergraduates) willing to mentor during the summer.
- You can produce a **steady stream of testable questions**, for example from a course you teach or computational work in the lab.
- You can commit to **multiple years**. The multi-year structure is the core of the model.

If one of these isn't true yet, start small: a single pair of local students, for one summer, with a plan to return. See the low-cost version in [Funding and budget](04-funding-and-budget.md).
