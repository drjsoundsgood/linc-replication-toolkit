# 12 · Publishing with high school students

Co-authorship on a peer-reviewed paper is one of LiNC's most powerful outcomes. It validates students as scientists, strengthens college applications, and tells students in the clearest possible way that their work counted.

## How the manuscript comes together

In the course-linked model (see [Connecting a course to the summer](06-course-to-lab-link.md)):

1. **Spring:** graduate students' written reports from the course, written as manuscripts, form the **skeleton**: introduction, computational methods and predictions.
2. **Summer:** LiNC students' experiments **test the predictions** and supply the experimental results.
3. **Fall:** LiNC students help write the experimental methods, results and discussion, with mentors guiding and editing.
4. **Submission:** the PI assembles, edits and submits the manuscript.

### Example
Bose E, *et al.*, Jones AN. *Bioinformatic and experimental characterization of the RBM15 RNA binding protein.* Journal of Structural Biology (2025). [doi:10.1016/j.jsb.2025.108264](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsb.2025.108264). This paper includes high school co-authors from the founding LiNC cohort alongside graduate students from the spring course.

## Authorship

Set expectations at the start of the summer:

- Students who **generate data that appears in the paper and contribute to writing or revising it** are co-authors.
- Follow your field's authorship standards (for example the [ICMJE criteria](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html) or [CRediT contributor roles](https://credit.niso.org/)) and apply them the same way to every contributor.
- Large author lists are normal for course-based papers. Use a contributions statement so each person's role is clear.

## Expect it to take more than one summer

Not every summer produces a paper. Often it takes **several years, and several cohorts,** before a project is complete enough to publish. Data from one summer's students may be combined with data from the next summer's, and with work from lab members during the year.

Tell students this from the start, so a summer without a paper doesn't feel like a failure. Keep careful records of who generated which data, so students from earlier cohorts are included as co-authors when the paper comes out, even if they've already left for college.

## Practical points

- **Consent:** confirm with students and families that students may be named as authors. Your program office may have a process.
- **Contact details:** use institutional affiliation (your lab) for students; don't publish personal contact information for minors.
- **Timelines:** publication can take a year or more. Keep students informed at each stage: submission, reviews, revision, acceptance. Each stage is a lesson in how science works.
- **Preprints:** posting a preprint lets students cite their work in college applications before formal publication.
- **Celebrate:** when the paper is published, celebrate with the students and their families.

## Other ways to share results

While a paper is in progress, students can share their work through:
- Posters at local or regional meetings, or high school science fairs
- Talks at partner institutions
- Undergraduate and high school research journals
- Program websites or newsletters (with consent)
